Airline Flight Delay Prediction & Analysis

Using only the information available around departure, can we tell whether a flight will arrive 15 or more minutes late? A full pipeline across 518,368 U.S. domestic flight records, ending in a Streamlit prediction app and a four-page Power BI report.

01 — Problem

Delays are expensive, and they cascade

Flight delays create real operational and customer-service problems for airlines and airports. Knowing that a flight is going to land late while it is still on the ground is worth more than knowing it after the fact — crews, gates and connecting passengers can all be re-planned.

So the project was framed as a prediction question rather than a reporting one: can we predict, using information available around departure, whether an operating flight will arrive 15 or more minutes late?

02 — Objective

What the project set out to do

  • Identify the carrier, route and operational factors associated with arrival delays.
  • Provide clear visual evidence answering the key business questions about delays.
  • Predict arrival-delay risk as a binary classification problem.
  • Compare models and select one on defined criteria rather than on accuracy alone.
  • Deploy the result as an interactive tool (Streamlit) alongside a business dashboard (Power BI).

A success criterion was fixed before modelling began: F1-score ≥ 0.70.

03 — Dataset

One month of U.S. domestic operations

The source file is T_ONTIME_REPORTING.csv, covering U.S. domestic flight operations for January 2026.

The target is imbalanced, which is why accuracy alone was never going to be a sufficient measure of success — a model that predicts "on time" for everything already scores around 80%.

04 — Data preparation

Deciding what the model is allowed to know

Cancelled flights were removed first: the delay target is artificial for a flight that never operated. That step took the working set from 544,003 rows to 518,368.

Removing data leakage

The most important decision was cutting columns the model could not legitimately have at prediction time. ARR_DELAY, ACTUAL_ELAPSED_TIME, TAXI_OUT and Arrival Time Block are all only known during or after the flight. Leaving any of them in would let the model cheat and produce a score that would collapse the moment it met a real flight.

Removing non-predictive identifiers

Tail numbers, flight numbers and duplicate airport, city and state columns carry no generalisable signal and were dropped.

Feature engineering and encoding

  • Departure Hour extracted from the scheduled departure time.
  • Low-frequency airports grouped into an Other bucket, keeping the top 20, then one-hot encoded along with carrier, origin and destination.
  • Train/test split of 80/20, stratified to preserve the class balance.
  • StandardScaler fitted on the training set only — scaling on the full dataset is leakage of a subtler kind.

The lesson that stuck

Identifying and removing the leakage columns was the real technical challenge of this project. A model that looks excellent because it can already see the answer is worse than useless — it is confidently wrong in production. Being strict about what is knowable at prediction time is the part of the work that actually determines whether a model is worth deploying.

05 — Exploratory analysis

What the data said before any model did

  • Departure delay is the single strongest signal for arrival delay (correlation ≈ 0.49).
  • Delay rate varies widely by carrier. Highest: JetBlue Airways (32.7%), Spirit Airlines (28.8%), Frontier Airlines (28.6%). Lowest: Southwest Airlines (14.9%), Republic Airways (15.2%), Envoy Air (16.3%).
  • Detroit (DTW) has the highest airport delay rate at 30.7%, ahead of major hubs including Chicago O'Hare and New York JFK.
  • Delay rate climbs steadily through the day — lowest around 5 AM, highest in the evening. This is consistent with delay propagation: a late aircraft pushes its own later flights late.
  • Flight distance has almost no correlation with delay (≈ 0.03). Delay is driven by operational conditions, not trip length.

06 — Model comparison

Two models, judged on the cost of being wrong

Performance comparison of Logistic Regression and Decision Tree on the held-out test set
Model Accuracy Precision Recall F1-score
Logistic Regression (selected) 92.19% 89.03% 71.10% 79.06%
Decision Tree (max_depth=8) 92.22% 90.57% 69.74% 78.80%

Why Logistic Regression was chosen

The Decision Tree is marginally ahead on accuracy and precision. Logistic Regression was still selected, for three reasons:

  • Best F1-score (79.06% vs 78.80%) — the metric the success criterion was written against.
  • Best recall (71.10% vs 69.74%). Missing a real delay is operationally more costly than raising a false alarm, so recall is the metric that matters most here.
  • Interpretability. Coefficients can be read directly, which makes the model explainable to people who have to act on it.

The pre-defined success criterion of F1 ≥ 0.70 was met and exceeded at 0.79.

Top predictive features

  • DEP_DELAY — by far the strongest predictor.
  • Destination airport — e.g. ORD / Chicago O'Hare, a major hub.
  • Operating carrier — e.g. Southwest Airlines associated with lower delay risk.
  • Origin airport — e.g. DTW / Detroit.

07 — Deployment

Putting the model where it can be used

A Streamlit web app loads the trained model and returns live predictions. The model, the scaler and the feature-column list are all persisted with joblib and loaded at startup, so nothing is retrained at runtime.

The app has three tabs:

  • Prediction — enter carrier, origin, destination, distance and departure hour, and get a delay probability back.
  • Insights — the EDA charts behind the model.
  • Model Performance — metrics and the model comparison, so the numbers stay visible rather than buried in a notebook.

08 — Business intelligence view

Flight Operations Analytics

A separate Power BI report was built on the same cleaned dataset, covering four analysis pages behind a branded landing page. Where the model answers "will this flight be late?", the report answers "where are the delays coming from, and who is affected?".

A reporting judgement worth flagging

Several routes in the route table show a 100% delay rate. These are low-volume routes where a handful of flights drives the figure, so they are worth flagging as small-sample cases rather than presenting as a genuine ranking of the worst routes.

09 — Results

Where it landed

  • 0.79 F1-score (criterion ≥ 0.70)
  • 71.10% Recall on delayed flights
  • 518,368 Records modelled
  • 2 Deployed outputs

What I learned

  • Leakage is a modelling decision, not a data-cleaning afterthought. Deciding what the model is allowed to see is the step that determines whether the score means anything.
  • Metric choice has to follow from the cost of the error. Picking the model with the lower accuracy was the right call once recall was recognised as the operationally expensive metric.
  • A predictive view and a reporting view answer different questions on the same data, and both are needed — one to act on a single flight, one to see the pattern across a month.
  • Scaling fitted on the full dataset is leakage too. Fitting on train only is a small habit with real consequences.
